## 2. Failure modes

### 2.1 Overconfidence-driven excessive trading
- **Evidence:** S1 — most-active-trading households earned 11.4%/yr vs. 17.9% market return, on 75%/yr turnover; S2 — men trade 45% more than women and lose 2.65 pp/yr vs. 1.72 pp/yr for women; S4 — bought stocks underperform sold stocks by ~3.3 pp; S12 — overconfident/sensation-seeking investors trade more with no performance gain.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** brokers (commissions on the extra 75%+/yr turnover) and the market makers / limit-order providers who take the other side of each additional round-trip; not necessarily any single identifiable informed trader — much of it is transferred to whoever is providing liquidity at that moment.
- **The inverse:** to sit on the other side of overconfident retail flow, an account must repeatedly take the liquidity-providing (patient, low-turnover) side across many separate counterparties' trades and hold conviction rather than churn — a stance about trade *frequency and selectivity*, not inherently about selling, overnight exposure, or shares; a low-turnover long-only 0DTE debit approach can partially avoid being the overconfident trader but does not itself capture the churned commissions.
- **Caveats:** U.S. discount-brokerage retail sample, 1990s equities, pre-zero-commission era — trading costs were higher then, so the return gap partly reflects an environment with steeper commissions than exist today; the effect is about excess trading volume, not necessarily about skill.

### 2.2 Disposition effect (selling winners, holding losers)
- **Evidence:** S3 — investors show "a strong preference for realizing winners rather than losers," unexplained by rebalancing, cost avoidance, or superior subsequent performance, and costly after tax; S19 — the resulting post-announcement underreaction is tradeable, yielding "monthly alphas of over 200 basis points" to those who trade against it.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** other, informed/momentum-style traders who exploit the predictable underreaction the disposition effect creates (S19's event-driven strategy); for the taxable-account version, also the tax authority (in the sense of avoidable tax paid from suboptimal loss/gain timing).
- **The inverse:** capturing the Frazzini (S19) alpha requires holding a position through the multi-week/month drift window around a news event and, for full expression of the strategy, taking positions on both sides of the news (i.e., shorting the side the disposition-affected holders are underreacting to) — this requires overnight holding and, for the short leg, selling, both excluded for the target account.
- **Caveats:** S3's sample is U.S. discount-brokerage accounts from the 1990s; S19 uses mutual-fund holdings as a proxy for reference prices, an indirect measure of individual investors' actual cost basis. Both papers describe a behavioral bias, not a claim that any given individual investor's return is fully explained by it.

### 2.3 Persistent day-trading unprofitability (most day traders lose, only a tiny minority is skilled)
- **Evidence:** S8 — 97% of Brazilian futures day traders who persisted >300 days lost money, only 1.1% beat minimum wage; S9 — "about twice as many day traders lose money as make money," ~20% "more than marginally profitable"; S7 — 80% of Taiwanese day traders quit within 2 years, <1% predictably profitable; S6 — the top 500 ranked day traders earn 37.9 bps/day after fees while the bottom-ranked lose 28.9 bps/day after fees.
- **Class:** statistical-method
- **Who profits:** the small, persistent skilled minority identified in S6/S7 captures returns from the much larger population of unprofitable day traders (day trading in an order-driven market is close to zero-sum before costs, and negative-sum after costs); brokers/exchanges also take a cut in fees regardless of who wins.
- **The inverse:** to be structurally on the winning side requires being in that <1%–20% with a real, persistent statistical edge — i.e., a genuine forecasting edge repeated across many trades, which is a property of the strategy's edge itself, not of selling, overnight holding, or share ownership; in principle compatible with a long-only 0DTE structure if a true edge exists, but the base rate evidence says such edges are rare and were identified in these studies only in hindsight, over years of data.
- **Caveats:** S8's population is exhaustive (all new entrants, not a survivor-biased sample) — a rare and valuable design feature; S6/S7/S9 samples are Taiwan (1992–2006) and one U.S. firm (2000s), both order-driven markets with different cost/rebate structures than modern U.S. equity/options markets — magnitudes should not be assumed to transfer directly.

### 2.4 Cost and spread drag from crossing the bid-ask spread
- **Evidence:** S5 — aggregate Taiwanese individual-investor annual performance penalty of 3.8 pp, with losses "traced to aggressive orders" while passive orders are profitable at short horizons; S21 — average option trade return of -0.93% against a typical quoted spread of 3.7%.
- **Class:** structural-cost
- **Who profits:** market makers / liquidity providers and brokers who capture the spread whenever a retail order crosses it (market/marketable-limit orders), plus any payment-for-order-flow arrangements built on that spread capture.
- **The inverse:** to be on the capturing side of the spread requires posting resting (passive) orders that other participants cross, i.e., acting as a liquidity provider rather than a liquidity taker — this is achievable with limit orders alone and does not require selling, overnight holding, or share ownership; however, true two-sided market-making (quoting both a bid and an offer) does require the ability to sell, which the target account cannot do.
- **Caveats:** S5's "passive orders profitable at short horizons" finding is about Taiwanese equity limit orders, not options; S21's spread/return numbers are for U.S. retail options 2020–2022 and are an average across a large, heterogeneous population of trades, not a like-for-like comparison controlling for strategy.

### 2.5 Adverse selection on aggressive/marketable orders
- **Evidence:** S5 — "virtually all of individual trading losses can be traced to their aggressive orders."
- **Class:** structural-cost
- **Who profits:** the (typically more informed, or simply faster/better-positioned) counterparties who fill the aggressive order — market makers and other informed traders who are willing to take the other side precisely because the incoming order is marketable.
- **The inverse:** requires being the counterparty willing to fill marketable retail orders, i.e., again a liquidity-provision (passive/resting order) posture rather than crossing the spread — actionable within a long-only, no-overnight structure by using limit rather than market orders; it does not by itself require selling, overnight exposure, or shares.
- **Caveats:** Evidence is specific to the Taiwan Stock Exchange's order-driven market structure; the mechanism (adverse selection against aggressive orders) is a general market-microstructure result but the exact magnitude (3.8 pp) is not shown to generalize to options or to U.S. markets.

### 2.6 0DTE / short-dated options as a structurally unfavorable product for buyers
- **Evidence:** S20 — retail 0DTE index-option traders lost a collective "$358,000 a day" since daily expirations began in May 2022, over "$125 million" cumulatively; S21 — 0DTE trades underperform non-0DTE option trades by 4.7 percentage points (t = -10) while non-0DTE trades earn +0.19%; option purchases (debit trades) lose 3.95% on average while naked option sales (13% of all trades) earn +20%.
- **Class:** product-design
- **Who profits:** per S21, the sell side of the options market (naked option writers) captures the average gain that option buyers give up; more broadly, options market makers/wholesalers who internalize and price retail order flow (the mechanism ESMA/FCA-type disclosures do not cover, since those concern CFDs, not exchange-listed options) sit on the other side of most retail 0DTE debit flow.
- **The inverse:** on this evidence, being on the historically profitable side of 0DTE option flow means **selling/writing options** (going short premium) rather than buying them — this is the single most direct conflict with the target account's constraints, since the account is mandated long-only (debit trades only), 0DTE, no selling. The evidence in S20/S21 describes exactly the trade type (0DTE, debit, index options) the target account is restricted to, on the historically losing side of that specific flow.
- **Caveats:** S20 and S21 are both 2023–2024 SSRN working papers on U.S. index/equity 0DTE options in the 2020–2022(S20: post-May-2022) window — not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed and published in a journal at the time of this search; the "naked sales earn 20%" figure is an average return across all naked sales in the sample and is not risk-adjusted for the tail risk naked selling carries.

### 2.7 Gambling / lottery preference in stock and option selection
- **Evidence:** S11 — investors overweighting lottery-type (positively skewed) stocks "typically earned 2 to 3 percent less than other investors," with the effect concentrated among lower-income investors.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** partly deadweight (loss from systematically overpaying for statistically overpriced skewness, with no single counterparty capturing the whole loss), and partly whoever sells/issues the overpriced lottery-like security to the retail buyer.
- **The inverse:** capturing the lottery-mispricing premium identified in S11 requires taking the other side of the skew — i.e., selling/shorting the overpriced lottery-type security — which is excluded for a long-only account; a long-only account can only avoid this failure mode (by not buying instruments selected for their skew/lottery appeal) rather than capture the premium.
- **Caveats:** S11's ~70,000-investor sample and period are not fully specified in the retrieved summaries; the 2–3% figure comes from secondary science-press coverage of the peer-reviewed Journal of Finance article rather than being independently confirmed from the primary-text abstract in this search.

### 2.8 Attention-/sentiment-driven buying (chasing what is visible)
- **Evidence:** S13 — intense Robinhood-user buying in a stock forecasts a -4.7% average 20-day abnormal return, and Robinhood outages disproportionately cut trading in high-attention names; S12 — sensation-seeking/overconfident investors (proxied by speeding-ticket records) trade more without any performance benefit.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** sellers into the attention-driven price spike (which can include informed investors, patient long-term holders realizing gains, or market makers unwinding inventory at temporarily favorable prices).
- **The inverse:** capturing the -4.7%/20-day effect requires shorting (selling) into the attention spike and holding the short over roughly a 20-trading-day window — both selling and overnight holding, excluded for the target account; the account can only avoid the loss (by not chasing high-attention buying) rather than capture the documented reversal.
- **Caveats:** S13's sample is Robinhood users specifically (2018–2020), a demographically distinct subset of retail; the -4.7% figure is an average abnormal return over 20 days for the *most*-bought stocks each day, not a claim about every attention-driven trade.

### 2.9 Correlated ("herding") retail order flow
- **Evidence:** S17 — 37,000 individual investors at a German broker tend to be "on the same side of the market" in the same stock over the same day/week/month/quarter; correlated market orders and correlated limit orders both predict subsequent returns.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** counterparties (institutional or other informed traders) who anticipate or trade against the correlated retail flow and its associated price pressure.
- **The inverse:** trading against a whole retail cohort's correlated flow, per S17, plays out over horizons up to a quarter — implying multi-day to multi-month holding, i.e., overnight exposure, which conflicts with the target account's no-overnight constraint.
- **Caveats:** effect sizes in S17 are reported directionally (correlation and predictive significance); a single summary percentage magnitude was not retrieved in this search (see Section 4). Sample is a single German discount broker's clients in the 1990s–2000s.

### 2.10 Wealth transfer from unsophisticated to sophisticated investors in bubble-crash cycles
- **Evidence:** S18 — in China's 2014–2015 equity bubble and crash, the largest 0.5% of households gained while the bottom 85% lost 250 billion RMB through active trading, equal to 30% of either group's initial equity wealth.
- **Class:** behavioural
- **Who profits:** the top 0.5% of households by portfolio size — larger, presumably better-resourced or better-informed investors who bought and sold at the more favorable points in the cycle.
- **The inverse:** being on the winning side, per S18, means being a large, well-capitalized household that sells into the bubble peak and/or buys the crash trough, holding equity positions through weeks/months of the cycle — this requires holding shares overnight and both buying and selling, none of which the target account (options, no shares, no overnight) can directly replicate.
- **Caveats:** single-country, single-episode (China, one specific bubble-crash) administrative dataset; whether the same redistribution mechanism and magnitude recurs in other bubbles/markets is not established by this one paper alone.

### 2.11 Excess leverage amplifying losses
- **Evidence:** S10 — the U.S. 2010 retail-FX leverage cap "reduces trading volume by 23%, alleviates high-leverage traders' losses by 40%, and reduces brokerages' operating capital by 25%," without changing the bid-ask prices brokers charge.
- **Class:** risk-management
- **Who profits:** brokers/dealers earn from spread and financing charges roughly independent of client leverage or outcome (per S10, relative bid-ask prices did not change with the leverage cap); at the individual-trade level, whichever counterparty is on the other side of a leveraged position that is forced to liquidate (e.g., via margin call) captures the liquidation flow.
- **The inverse:** this failure mode is about position sizing/leverage, not about market side — a long-only, no-overnight, capped-daily-loss (3–5%) account structure directly targets the mechanism S10 identifies (leverage amplifying losses) without needing to sell, hold overnight, or hold shares.
- **Caveats:** S10 studies retail FX specifically, a different product and regulatory regime than 0DTE listed options; the causal estimate is from a single regulatory change (the 2010 U.S. leverage cap) and its generalizability to other leverage contexts (e.g., options buying power) is not established by this paper.

### 2.12 CFD/leveraged-product design in which most retail accounts lose (regulatory disclosure evidence)
- **Evidence:** S15 — ESMA: "74-89% of retail [CFD] accounts typically lose money," average loss per client €1,600–€29,000; S16 — FCA: "82% of clients lost money" on CFDs, average loss per client account £2,200.
- **Class:** product-design
- **Who profits:** CFD providers, many of which act as principal counterparty to client positions (in addition to, or instead of, merely earning spread/commission) — i.e., the broker itself is frequently the direct counterparty capturing the client's loss in the CFD product structure these disclosures cover.
- **The inverse:** capturing this specific edge means being the CFD dealer/broker acting as principal counterparty to retail clients — a business-model position, not a trading strategy a client account can adopt; it does not map onto "sell/hold overnight/hold shares" choices available to an end-user trading account.
- **Caveats:** S15/S16 concern CFDs and binary options specifically (leveraged OTC derivative products), not exchange-listed 0DTE options; the loss percentages reflect the CFD product's structure (financing costs, spread, leverage) and provider business model, which differ from a listed-options long-only account, so the magnitudes should not be assumed to transfer directly to 0DTE options.

## 3. Cross-cutting numbers

Numbers that recur, in different populations/periods, across the sources above:

- **Share of active/day traders that lose money:** roughly two-thirds to nearly all, depending on population — S9 (~2x as many day traders lose as win; ~20% "more than marginally profitable"), S7 (80% of day traders quit within 2 years; <1% predictably profitable), S8 (97% of persistent Brazilian day traders lose money), S15 (74–89% of retail CFD accounts lose), S16 (82% of retail CFD accounts lose).
- **Magnitude of annual underperformance from trading:** on the order of a few percentage points per year — S1 (11.4% vs. 17.9% market, a 6.5 pp gap, for the most active traders), S2 (2.65 pp/yr for men vs. 1.72 pp/yr for women), S5 (aggregate 3.8 pp/yr performance penalty, Taiwan), S11 (2–3%/yr for lottery-stock overweighters).
- **Cost/spread share of the loss:** S5 (losses "traced to aggressive orders," i.e., spread-crossing), S21 (average option trade -0.93% vs. 3.7% typical quoted spread — the spread is several times the average realized loss, implying spread cost dominates), S1 (75%/yr portfolio turnover multiplying transaction-cost drag).
- **0DTE-specific losses (most relevant to a long-only 0DTE account):** S20 ($358,000/day collective retail loss on 0DTE index options since May 2022, >$125 million cumulative) and S21 (0DTE trades underperform non-0DTE trades by 4.7 pp; option purchases/debit trades lose 3.95% on average while naked sales earn +20%) point the same direction — 0DTE, and specifically 0DTE buying (debit trades), is the segment of the options market with the largest documented average retail loss.
