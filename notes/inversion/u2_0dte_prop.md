# U2 — 0DTE options and prop-firm rules: who wins, who loses, and why

Scope note: this file covers (1) retail 0DTE/options failure modes, (2) day- and swing-trading failure modes generally (added per coordinator scope change, section 2b), and (3) prop-firm rule-induced failure modes. Per coordination with unit U1, Barber-Odean's core turnover/overconfidence papers, the Taiwan and Brazil day-trader datasets, Kumar's lottery-stock work, Grinblatt-Keloharju, and the FCA/ESMA CFD retail-loss disclosures are U1's primary territory — they are named here only where directly relevant and are not re-derived at length. Evidence only; no trading advice. Every number below is attributed to a retrieved URL; where a number could not be confirmed it is marked UNVERIFIED and is not used elsewhere in this file.

Target-account context used throughout: 0DTE options only, LONG-ONLY (buy calls/puts only), NO SELLING, NO SPREADS, NO SHARES, NO OVERNIGHT HOLDS, daily loss limit 3–5%. Wherever the literature's "winning side" requires selling premium, holding overnight, or trading shares, that is flagged explicitly, because this account cannot occupy that side by construction.

## 2. Failure modes in retail options and 0DTE

**Mode 1 — Effective bid-ask spread captured by a concentrated set of wholesalers**
Evidence: S01 (avg. 12.6% bid-ask spread on retail-preferred cheap/weekly options; ~90% of PFOF to three wholesalers; retail >60% of options volume); S07 (even after adjusting for execution timing, average effective spread still ~25% below "conventional" estimate — implying a real, non-trivial residual cost, larger still for traders who do not time entries); S31 (per-order PFOF rates disclosed by Robinhood, 28–42.7¢/100 shares-equivalent to Citadel Securities, Oct 2024).
Class: structural-cost.
Who profits: the "big three" options wholesalers (per S01, largely Citadel Securities, Susquehanna, and Wolverine) via the bid-ask spread they capture internalizing retail flow, plus the PFOF rebate paid to the broker (S31) that is itself funded by that spread.
The inverse: to sit on this side you must be a continuously-quoting, two-sided market maker willing to be short (or long) any given 0DTE contract at any moment the retail order arrives, and to warehouse/hedge that inventory — i.e., you must be a designated liquidity provider, not a directional buyer. A long-only intraday buyer cannot be on this side at all: by definition it only ever crosses the spread, never earns it. [Requires SELLING/market-making.]
Caveats: S07 shows sophisticated execution timing recovers a large share of the naive spread cost — the loss is concentrated in unsophisticated order entry, not an unconditional tax on all retail flow.

**Mode 2 — Overpaying implied volatility ahead of anticipated events that don't fully materialize**
Evidence: S02 (retail option buyers "overpay for options relative to realized volatility," "incur enormous bid-ask spreads," and "sluggishly respond to announcements"; losses of 5–9% on average, 10–14% for high-expected-volatility announcements).
Class: behavioural + statistical-method.
Who profits: whoever sold the pre-event implied-volatility richness and is short vega/theta into the event — i.e., option writers (often market makers laying off risk, or option-selling traders) who collect the gap between elevated implied volatility and lower realized volatility.
The inverse: must be short options (short vega) into the anticipated event and typically must hold the position through the event window itself, since single-stock earnings are announced outside continuous trading hours (after close / before open). [Requires SELLING and typically OVERNIGHT.] A long-only, no-overnight 0DTE buyer cannot structurally occupy this side; it can at most buy options on the announcement day itself, which is a related but distinct exposure from the pre-event vol-richness trade S02 studies.
Caveats: S02's population is single-stock options around earnings, not literally SPX 0DTE (the index has no discrete EPS announcements); the mechanism (paying an implied-volatility premium that isn't repaid by realized moves) generalizes conceptually to 0DTE per S05's variance-risk-premium framing, but S02's specific loss percentages should not be read as SPX-0DTE-specific numbers.

**Mode 3 — Long-only "lottery" positioning while the market's own documented "sophisticated" retail flow is short-premium**
Evidence: S11 (95% of SPX 0DTE retail trades use capped-risk strategies; >50% of SPX 0DTE retail notional is multi-leg; dominant retail strategies are short put verticals, short call verticals, and short iron condors — "rather than long-call lottery bets"; complex-order share of notional 58–77% of C1, Q4 2019–Q4 2023); S08 (typical retail trade across a broad $15bn dataset is a one-day SPX call bought and held about an hour; "little evidence of positive skewness" in realized dollar profits, i.e., no evidence the lottery framing pays off); S03 (aggregate $241,000/day retail loss in an SPX 0DTE population dominated by short-dated buying).
Class: product-design + behavioural.
Who profits: within the short-premium cohort, profit flows to whichever side is short when the option expires worthless (the premium seller); for the long-only naked-option cohort, profit flows to whoever wrote the contract (again the seller/market maker), captured via decay when the long option is not deep in the money at expiry.
The inverse: to run the pattern Cboe's own data shows dominates "sophisticated" SPX 0DTE retail flow requires selling options / running defined-risk multi-leg spreads (short verticals, iron condors). [Requires SELLING and SPREADS — both explicitly forbidden for the target account.] A long-only, no-spreads account is mechanically confined to the minority, lottery-style segment of retail 0DTE flow that S08 finds does not even show the compensating positive skew a lottery ticket would need to be worth its cost.
Caveats: S11 documents strategy composition (what retail does), not the profitability of each strategy type; "capped risk"/"multi-leg" is not the same claim as "profitable." S08's "modest losses" framing is milder than S03's large aggregate-dollar-loss framing — the two papers use different datasets/populations (broad multi-underlying retail flow vs. SPX-specific 0DTE flow) and are not strictly comparable.

**Mode 4 — Ultra-short-tenor mispricing disadvantage relative to dealer-grade models**
Evidence: S05 (a purpose-built Edgeworth-expansion model materially improves pricing and hedging of 0DTE options versus "state-of-the-art models," and the same paper reports "nearly instantaneous predictability" of 0DTE-based variance risk premia once modeled correctly).
Class: statistical-method.
Who profits: participants with access to (or capable of building) short-tenor-specific pricing/hedging models — i.e., the dealers and sophisticated market-making desks the paper's model is designed to emulate — who can identify and monetize the skew/kurtosis mispricing that "state-of-the-art" (i.e., off-the-shelf) models miss at ultra-short tenors.
The inverse: requires proprietary short-tenor pricing infrastructure and the ability to quote/hedge both sides of the market. [Effectively requires SELLING/market-making capacity, not a directional retail order.] A retail long-only buyer has no comparable pricing edge and is, by the paper's own framing, on the less-informed side of that mispricing by default.
Caveats: the paper is about pricing/hedging model performance, not a direct measurement of retail P&L; the "who loses" side is an inference, not something S05 measures directly.

**Mode 5 — Attention- and event-driven purchase timing**
Evidence: S24 (intense Robinhood buying in a stock forecasts "average 20-day abnormal returns of -4.7%" for the top-bought names each day — an equities finding, cited for the timing mechanism); S03 (retail's revealed preference for 0DTE precisely because of "lottery-like payoffs and perceived potential for generating quick profits").
Class: behavioural.
Who profits: whoever is on the other side of the attention-driven order flow at the moment of the spike — informed sellers or market makers who can mark up prices/spreads into a temporary demand surge (folds back into Mode 1's spread-capture channel).
The inverse: being the counterparty who sells into a retail attention spike (again, a market-making / selling role). [Requires SELLING.] Not directly available to a long-only buyer.
Caveats: S24's headline number is measured in equities (shares bought on Robinhood), not options; it is cited here only for the underlying attention mechanism, which S03 documents separately and directly for 0DTE option choice.

**Mode 6 — Belief in a harvestable "dealer short-gamma" edge that the index-level evidence does not support**
Evidence: S04 ("high open interest gamma in 0DTEs does not propagate past volatility"; 0DTE volume shocks "do not amplify recent past index returns"); S12 ("no uptick in intraday gap moves," "market maker net exposure is fairly negligible," "market volatility is lower on days when 0DTE options are available for trading," attributed to balanced two-sided 0DTE flow).
Class: statistical-method + risk-management.
Who profits: no one systematically, according to this evidence — this mode is a myth-correction rather than a wealth transfer: retail narratives (and some trading services) built around anticipating dealer gamma-driven feedback loops in SPX 0DTE are not supported as a reliable, repeatable phenomenon at the index level by Cboe's own research or by S04.
The inverse: not applicable as a repeatable trade for either side at the SPX-index level; see Section 4 for the one documented single-name counter-example (GameStop, 2021).
Caveats: this contradicts a popular practitioner narrative (dealer short-gamma "squeezes" as a tradeable 0DTE signal); both S04 and S12 are relatively recent, single-source-family (Cboe-adjacent or Cboe-authored in S12's case) findings and should be weighed against the possibility of house-view bias in S12 specifically, since Cboe has a commercial interest in 0DTE volume being seen as non-destabilizing.

**Mode 7 — Time-decay (theta) acceleration compressed into hours rather than days**
Evidence: S03 (0DTE-specific retail loss magnitude, $241,000/day average, in an instrument whose entire remaining time value must decay to zero within the same session); S05 (0DTE-specific pricing/hedging model needed precisely because standard longer-dated approximations misstate the skew/kurtosis and, implicitly, the decay path, at ultra-short tenors).
Class: structural-cost + risk-management.
Who profits: the option writer on the other side of each long position, who collects the full time-value decay whenever the underlying fails to move enough, fast enough, in the buyer's favor before expiry — a decay curve that is steeper and more front-loaded in the final hour(s) than in a multi-day option.
The inverse: to be paid this decay you must be short the option (the writer). [Requires SELLING.] Not available to a long-only buyer, who can only try to be right about direction and magnitude within an increasingly short and adverse time window.
Caveats: evidence here is indirect (inferred from aggregate loss figures and pricing-model motivation) rather than a direct, published decomposition of loss into a "theta" line item; see Section 4 and Section 5 for the explicit statement that no clean spread/decay/direction decomposition was found.

**Mode 8 — Trading concentrated at the open, on overnight-stale information, in the most crowded liquidity window**
Evidence: S27 (retail options trades "cluster near market opens," more so for attention-sensitive mini-options, with "less positive intraday returns and more negative overnight returns" than standard-option investors); S11 (majority of retail SPX 0DTE volume runs through complex/multi-leg orders that are commonly entered near the open to establish the day's defined-risk structure).
Class: behavioural + structural.
Who profits: liquidity providers who are typically quoting their widest effective spreads right at the open (when order imbalance and information asymmetry are highest), capturing more of Mode 1's spread on open-clustered orders than on orders spread evenly through the session.
The inverse: being the compensated liquidity provider at the open requires quoting, i.e., being willing to be short optionality at that moment. [Requires SELLING.] A long-only buyer entering at the open is, on this evidence, entering into the structurally most expensive part of the session on average.
Caveats: S27's own dataset is mini-options (a narrow, low-volume corner of the options market), not SPX 0DTE directly; the clustering finding is used here as directional evidence of a broader pattern, not as an SPX-0DTE-specific number.

## 2b. Failure modes in day and swing trading generally

**1. Overtrading / turnover erodes returns through repeated, avoidable transaction costs**
Evidence: S26 (SEBI: 93% of >1 crore individual India equity F&O traders lost money FY22–FY24, aggregate losses >₹1.8 lakh crore, average loss ~₹2 lakh/trader — a population defined by very high intraday/short-dated turnover); the foundational turnover-erodes-returns literature (Barber & Odean, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," and related work) is U1's primary territory and is named here rather than re-derived.
Class: behavioural.
Who profits: the exchange and broker via per-trade fees/transaction taxes, and the market-maker/counterparty side capturing the bid-ask spread on every extra round-trip generated by higher turnover.
The inverse: to profit from someone else's turnover you must be the fee-collecting intermediary or the standing liquidity provider capturing spread on each of their round-trips — not a fellow directional trader. [No SELLING/OVERNIGHT/SHARES requirement per se, but the winning role is structurally an intermediary, not a peer trader; a same-day, single-leg 0DTE buyer cannot avoid generating this cost on every entry and exit, since the account's own rules (0DTE, no overnight) force a full round-trip every single day.]
Caveats: S26 is a mixed futures-and-options population in the Indian market, not a pure options or pure 0DTE population; cited here as a large, regulator-verified analog to the turnover problem, distinct from the Taiwan/Brazil turnover datasets U1 covers.

**2. Disposition effect: cutting winners early, holding losers too long**
Evidence: S25 (Odean 1998: investors "1.5 to 2 times more likely to sell winning stocks than losers," not explained by rebalancing or tax motives in the way that would be optimal).
Class: behavioural.
Who profits: whoever is on the other side of the position the trader holds too long — for a long option position specifically, the passage of extra time while "hoping for a reversal" hands additional theta decay directly to the option's writer with no extra action required by that writer.
The inverse: none required beyond already being the writer of the position being held — the disposition mistake self-funds the counterparty automatically through time decay. [The "winning side" here is simply "the seller of the instrument," already covered under SELLING in Section 2's Mode 7; no separate share/overnight requirement.]
Caveats: Odean's original sample is equities, not options; the mechanism (reluctance to realize losses) is well documented across asset classes, but the exact magnitude ("1.5-2x") is an equities-account statistic, not an options-specific one — cited for the behavioural mechanism, not as an options number. Full treatment of Odean/Barber-Odean is U1's territory; included here only because the task explicitly names the disposition effect as a required mode.

**3. Overnight gap risk and the overnight-vs-intraday return split**
Evidence: S18 (Lou, Polk, Skouras: across 14 trading strategies, returns are earned "either entirely overnight... or entirely intraday," typically with opposite signs across the two components, i.e., an intraday-only trader systematically forfeits or fights against whichever component is not theirs).
Class: structural + statistical-method.
Who profits: whoever holds the exposure through the specific period (close-to-open, or open-to-close) that happens to be carrying the compensated return component for a given name/regime.
The inverse: capturing the overnight component requires holding a position through the close-to-next-open gap. [Requires OVERNIGHT — explicitly forbidden for the target account.] A no-overnight-holds account is, by rule, permanently confined to whichever sign the intraday component carries and cannot access the overnight component documented by S18 at all, in either direction.
Caveats: S18's findings are from broad-based equity strategies (momentum/reversal factors), not options or 0DTE specifically; the overnight/intraday split is a documented empirical regularity in the underlying, and its implications for a same-day options buyer are an inference (the buyer is exposed only to whatever intraday component exists on a given day), not a number S18 itself reports for options.

**4. Leverage and margin amplifying variance and losses**
Evidence: S19 (Heimer & Simsek: 2010 US retail-FX leverage cap reduced trading volume "by 23 percent," improved high-leverage traders' portfolio return "by 18 percentage points per month," alleviating losses "by 40 percent," and cut brokerage operating capital "by 25 percent," with no change in relative bid-ask spreads charged); S20 (ESMA, named briefly — 2018 EU CFD leverage cut from "30:1 to 2:1"; the associated CFD retail-loss-rate disclosures are U1's primary territory and are not re-derived here).
Class: structural + risk-management.
Who profits: brokerages/counterparties, whose realized economics (per S19, operating capital fell alongside the leverage cut, consistent with leverage-driven revenue) scale with the extra volume and variance that higher leverage generates in client accounts.
The inverse: no explicit SELLING/OVERNIGHT/SHARES requirement — the broker/counterparty profits structurally from higher-leverage clients' extra volume and blow-up rate rather than from taking a specific opposing position. For the target account, "leverage" is expressed as position size relative to the 3–5% daily loss limit on a decaying, binary-payoff instrument rather than as margined FX/CFD leverage; see Section 3 for the derived arithmetic linking position sizing directly to that daily loss limit.
Caveats: S19's population is retail FX, S20's is CFDs — neither is 0DTE options directly; cited for the general leverage-amplifies-losses mechanism, which the coordinator explicitly requested this unit cover.

**5. Loss-chasing / revenge trading after a losing streak**
Evidence: S29 (PnL App, practitioner data, labeled SURVEY: after a losing streak, traders "increase position size" and "increase how frequently they trade," attributed to loss aversion and an urge to recover losses quickly rather than a considered reassessment).
Class: behavioural.
Who profits: whoever is the counterparty on the incremental, lower-quality trades generated by the loss-chasing itself — for long-only 0DTE options, the option writer/wholesaler collecting additional spread (Mode 1) and additional decay (Mode 7) on each added revenge trade.
The inverse: being "the house" (market maker) on the extra flow generated by someone else's tilt. [Requires SELLING/market-making.] Not available to a long-only buyer, who can only manage this risk by not increasing size or frequency after losses.
Caveats: S29 is a practitioner/product blog citing its own users' data, not a peer-reviewed study; sample size, selection, and methodology are not disclosed on the retrieved page — treated as SURVEY-grade evidence, not VERIFIED-academic.

**6. Position sizing without a ruin constraint (Kelly criterion / variance drag) — DERIVED**
Evidence: S28 (Kelly 1956, theory only) grounds the following DERIVED arithmetic, which is not itself taken from any empirical retail-trading study.
DERIVED example: consider a trader with a genuinely positive-expectation bet — 50% probability of winning 1.5x the amount risked, 50% probability of losing the full amount risked, for a positive simple average return per trade of 0.5×1.5 + 0.5×(−1.0) = +0.25, i.e. +25% per trade on average. The Kelly-optimal fraction of capital to risk per trade on this bet is f* = 25%. If the trader instead risks a fixed fraction f = 75% of capital on every such trade (plausible for someone sizing 0DTE long options aggressively to hit a profit target fast), the expected per-trade *geometric* growth rate is 0.5·ln(1+0.75×1.5) + 0.5·ln(1−0.75×1.0) = 0.5·ln(2.125) + 0.5·ln(0.25) ≈ 0.5(0.754) + 0.5(−1.386) ≈ −0.316, i.e. capital shrinks toward zero on average over repeated trials even though every individual trade has a positive expected simple return. This is variance drag: a positive per-trade expectancy does not imply positive long-run compounded growth once bet size is large relative to the edge, and large enough oversizing produces near-certain eventual ruin regardless of edge sign.
Class: statistical-method + risk-management.
Who profits: no specific named counterparty — capital is destroyed by compounding volatility itself, though in a prop-firm context the forfeited capital effectively transfers to the firm via lost challenge fees (Section 3, item 4), and in the open market it transfers to whoever was the option seller each time a position is (over-)sized and lost.
The inverse: not applicable — this is a mathematical property of geometric compounding under oversized bet fractions, not a "someone must sell/hold overnight/hold shares" mechanism.
Caveats: the specific 50%/1.5x/loss-of-full-stake parameters are illustrative, chosen to make the arithmetic transparent, not measured from any cited dataset; the qualitative conclusion (oversizing causes ruin despite positive simple expectancy) is a standard, uncontested result of Kelly-criterion theory (S28), not itself an empirical finding about retail traders.

**7. Strategy-hopping and indicator/rule overfitting after losing stretches**
Evidence: S21 (Park & Irwin: of 95 "modern" technical-analysis studies surveyed, 56 positive, 20 negative, 19 mixed, with data-snooping and ex post rule selection flagged as pervasive problems); S22 (Sullivan, Timmermann, White: the best of ~7,846 trading rules on 100 years of DJIA data, chosen with the benefit of hindsight, "does not provide superior performance" in the subsequent 10-year out-of-sample period once data-snooping is corrected for, and shows no edge on S&P 500 futures either).
Class: statistical-method.
Who profits: brokers/exchanges (extra fees and spread from the extra trading volume generated each time a trader abandons one rule/indicator and tests another) and vendors of indicator/signal services; no informed trading counterparty is required for this mode specifically.
The inverse: not a "someone must sell/hold overnight/hold shares" mechanism — this is a pure statistical-inference failure (mistaking an in-sample-fit rule for a real edge). Each re-entry under a newly curve-fit rule still separately incurs Mode 1's spread cost, so strategy-hopping compounds Section 2's Mode 1 rather than creating a distinct counterparty channel.
Caveats: S21 and S22 study cross-asset technical trading generally (equities, FX, futures), not 0DTE options specifically or day/swing trading in isolation; cited for the general overfitting mechanism the coordinator asked this unit to cover.

**8. News and attention chasing**
Evidence: S23 (Barber & Odean, "All That Glitters," named per coordination with U1: individual investors are net buyers of attention-grabbing stocks); S24 (Barber, Huang, Odean, Schwarz: intense Robinhood buying in a name forecasts "average 20-day abnormal returns of -4.7%" for the day's most-bought stocks).
Class: behavioural.
Who profits: whoever sold into the attention-driven buying spike at the temporarily elevated price — informed sellers or market makers laying off inventory into retail demand.
The inverse: being the seller of shares into a retail attention spike. [The S24 finding itself is a SHARES/equity finding, not an options finding — flagged explicitly. A long-only 0DTE-options-only account cannot literally occupy or be directly harmed by this exact equity-selling mechanism, though the underlying behavioural driver — buying what is attention-grabbing, when it is grabbing attention — transfers directly to which SPX-linked 0DTE contracts a retail trader chooses to buy and when.]
Caveats: S23's core contribution and dataset are U1's primary territory; included here only briefly, for the timing-mechanism link to S24's more specific, more recent Robinhood number.

**9. Trading concentrated at the open, competing against the session's widest effective spreads**
Evidence: S27 (retail options trades cluster near market opens; mini-option retail investors show worse performance, more negative overnight returns, when trading is concentrated near the open); S11 (majority of SPX 0DTE retail notional runs through complex/multi-leg orders commonly established near the open).
Class: behavioural + structural.
Who profits: liquidity providers typically quoting wider effective spreads at the open (highest order-imbalance/information-asymmetry point of the session), capturing more of Section 2 Mode 1's spread on open-clustered orders.
The inverse: being the compensated liquidity provider at the open requires quoting/being willing to be short optionality at that moment. [Requires SELLING.] Not available to a long-only buyer.
Caveats: this mode duplicates Section 2's Mode 8 by design (0DTE options are a subset of day trading generally); repeated here only because the coordinator's day/swing list explicitly named "trading at the open" as a required mode, and S27's mini-options evidence is a distinct, non-0DTE-specific data point supporting it.

## 3. Failure modes induced by prop-firm rules

**Rule 1 — Daily loss limit (3–5% of account) forces small-N, binary-bet sizing — DERIVED**
The rule: a hard stop that ends trading (and often the account) for the day once losses reach 3–5% of starting balance/equity, consistent with the "4% and 5%" range multiple firms use per S30.
Behaviour it induces: because 0DTE long options frequently expire worthless (a full, discrete loss of the premium risked on that trade — this is the modal outcome for out-of-the-money short-dated options, consistent with S03/S08's framing of 0DTE as a short-holding-period, binary-ish payoff instrument), the daily loss limit effectively caps the number of independent, full-loss trades a trader can take before being forced out for the day.
Evidence / DERIVED arithmetic: let D = daily loss limit (as % of account) and x = % of account risked per trade (assuming, worst case, the position can go to a full loss of premium — a realistic 0DTE scenario). The maximum number of consecutive full-loss trades before breaching the limit is N = floor(D / x). At D = 4% and x = 1% per trade, N = 4: four consecutive worthless 0DTE buys and the account is done for the day, regardless of whether a fifth setup that day would have been profitable. At D = 4% and x = 2% per trade (larger size, faster to a decision), N = 2. This mechanically converts a strategy that might need, say, 8–10 attempts per day to find its edge (per S08's "held for an hour" typical retail 0DTE holding pattern, implying many short attempts are plausible in a session) into one that is statistically starved of trials on any day where the first few attempts go against the trader — independent of whether the underlying edge is real.
The inverse: none required beyond arithmetic — this is a self-imposed constraint interacting with instrument design, not a "someone else must sell/hold overnight/hold shares" mechanism; the "winner" is simply whoever is not subject to this constraint (the firm itself, or an unconstrained trader).

**Rule 2 — Trailing drawdown ratchets the risk budget tighter after every gain — DERIVED**
The rule: a floor that rises with the account's peak equity (sometimes peak *intraday* mark-to-market equity, sometimes only end-of-day closed equity) and never resets down, per S30's description of the intraday-vs-end-of-day distinction.
Behaviour it induces: a trader who has an intraday unrealized gain effectively raises their own floor before that gain is ever realized/banked, so a reversal that gives back an intraday gain can breach the rule even though the trader's loss relative to the day's *starting* balance is much smaller than the loss limit would suggest.
Evidence / DERIVED arithmetic: $100,000 account, 5% trailing maximum drawdown tracked on intraday equity (floor = peak equity − 5%). Trader is up $3,000 intraday (peak equity $103,000), so the floor becomes $103,000 − $5,000 = $98,000. If the position then reverses and equity falls to $97,800, the trailing-drawdown rule is breached — even though the account's loss relative to its $100,000 starting balance that morning is only $2,200 (a 2.2% day), well inside a nominal 5% daily loss limit. The trailing variant is thus strictly more restrictive than it appears from the headline percentage whenever it tracks intraday marks rather than closed, end-of-day equity.
The inverse: none required — a purely mechanical consequence of how the floor is defined, not a counterparty mechanism.
Caveats: whether a given firm's trailing drawdown tracks intraday or end-of-day equity varies by firm (S30); the arithmetic above illustrates the intraday-tracking case specifically, which is the stricter of the two common designs.

**Rule 3 — Consistency rules are mechanically hard to satisfy with a convex, fat-tailed payoff product — DERIVED**
The rule: caps the share of total profit that may come from a single day, commonly described as Consistency % = (Best Day Profit ÷ Total Profit) × 100, with caps "usually... between 15% and 50%" per S30.
Behaviour it induces: S11 documents that SPX 0DTE retail flow is dominated by capped-risk, often lottery-shaped payoffs; S08 finds a typical retail 0DTE trade is a short-duration, largely single-leg purchase. A strategy built from many small, frequently-worthless long-option purchases punctuated by rare large multiples (the shape S03's aggregate-loss and S08's skewness discussion both point toward, even if S08 disputes the skewness is positive on average) will, on any day it is profitable at all across a multi-day evaluation, tend to earn a disproportionate share of its cumulative profit on the day(s) a large multiple hits — precisely the pattern a consistency rule caps.
Evidence / DERIVED arithmetic: suppose across 10 trading days a strategy nets +$1,000 total profit, with $700 of that coming from one day that caught a large directional 0DTE move (a 30% cap example from S30's cited range) — Consistency % = $700/$1,000 = 70%, which breaches even a lenient 50% cap and would gate the payout, forcing the trader either to leave money on the table on their best day (stop early) or to manufacture additional smaller "profit days" specifically to dilute the ratio, neither of which is a response to genuine trading opportunity.
The inverse: none required — this is a structural mismatch between product payoff shape (documented convex/fat-tailed in the 0DTE literature) and rule design (penalizing exactly that shape), not a counterparty mechanism.
Caveats: the specific $700/$1,000 example is illustrative, not drawn from a cited dataset; the general point — that consistency rules interact adversely with lottery-shaped instruments — is a DERIVED inference from combining S11/S08's documented payoff shape with S30's rule-mechanics description, not itself a measured finding from any single source.

**Rule 4 — Evaluation-fee economics make the "challenge" product negative-expected-value for the typical entrant — DERIVED**
The rule: traders pay a fee (or series of fees) to attempt an evaluation; only those who pass, then remain funded, then hit payout criteria, ever receive money back.
Evidence / DERIVED arithmetic, using S16's own reported figures: average account spend is "$800" across an average of "three different challenges" per account lifecycle; "14%" pass; of those, "45%" achieve at least one payout (≈7% of all entrants); average payout is "4% of the plan size." If a typical funded plan size is (illustratively) $25,000–$100,000, 4% of that is $1,000–$4,000 per successful payout event. Expected payout per entrant ≈ 0.07 × [$1,000–$4,000] ≈ $70–$280, against an average spend of $800 per account — an expected value roughly −65% to −91% of the amount spent, for the median/average entrant, using S16's own reported rates.
The inverse: the firm collecting the $800-per-account average fee revenue from the ~93% of accounts (per S16's 7%-of-all-accounts-get-any-payout figure) that never earn a payout is the party with the favorable expected value in this specific transaction, independent of how any individual trader's market P&L nets out.
Caveats: S16 is a single vendor's dataset covering 10 firms, not an audited industry-wide census; plan-size and payout-amount ranges used above are illustrative interpolations of "4% of plan size," not a number S16 itself reports as a dollar figure; FTMO specifically (S17) discloses total payouts but not entrant counts, so this exact arithmetic cannot be replicated for FTMO from the sources retrieved.

**Rule 5 — Minimum trading days / time limits force trading on low-quality days to remain "active"**
The rule: "most firms require 5–10 trading days" of qualifying activity before a challenge can be passed, and some funded accounts are voided if "no trade is placed within a rolling 30-day window" (S30).
Behaviour it induces: a trader who reaches their profit target quickly, or who correctly judges that a given day offers no good setup, is nonetheless pushed to place a qualifying trade anyway to keep the count moving or avoid inactivity default — i.e., the rule can force trades that exist to satisfy a day-count rather than because a genuine opportunity was identified.
Evidence: S30 (rule description, cross-firm, industry-documentation grade, not an audited primary filing).
The inverse: none required — this is a self-imposed activity requirement, not a counterparty mechanism; the "winner" is again the firm (which benefits from more qualifying days generating more data/fee cycles) rather than a specific market counterparty.
Caveats: exact day counts and inactivity windows vary by firm; S30's description is a generalized synthesis across multiple firms' publicly stated rules, not one firm's audited disclosure.

**Rule 6 — The no-overnight / 0DTE-only mandate, combined with the daily loss limit, discards an entire documented return component**
The rule: this account may only trade options expiring same-day and may never hold overnight.
Behaviour it induces: per S18, a meaningful share of documented equity-strategy returns is concentrated specifically in the overnight (close-to-open) period, with the intraday period often carrying the opposite sign. An account barred from ever holding overnight is, by construction, confined to whatever the intraday component happens to be paying on any given day and cannot access the overnight component in either direction — it cannot benefit from it, but it also cannot be harmed by an overnight gap.
Evidence: S18 (14-strategy overnight/intraday decomposition, general equities); combined here with the account's own stated rules (not itself a market finding, but a direct structural consequence of combining S18's documented pattern with the account's no-overnight constraint).
The inverse: capturing the overnight component requires holding through the close-to-open gap. [Requires OVERNIGHT — explicitly forbidden.]
Caveats: S18 is not an options-specific or 0DTE-specific study; this rule-mode is a structural inference from combining a general equity-market regularity with the account's own constraints, not a direct measurement of 0DTE-specific overnight effects.

## 4. Counterparty economics of 0DTE in one page

**Who the natural sellers are.** Per S01, retail options order flow is overwhelmingly internalized by a small set of wholesalers — "nearly 90%" of payment for order flow in options goes to three firms — who act as the natural, continuous counterparty to retail buy orders, capturing the bid-ask spread (average 12.6% on the cheap, short-dated contracts retail prefers, per S01) as compensation for making a two-sided market. Separately, per S11, within retail's own 0DTE flow, over half of SPX 0DTE retail notional is itself short-premium (short verticals, short iron condors) — meaning some of the counterparty to a naked-long retail buyer is not a dealer at all but another, more risk-defined retail trader running the opposite structure.

**What they earn per contract per day, according to the sources retrieved.** The clearest documented figure is the per-order PFOF rate a wholesaler pays the broker for the right to internalize the order: S31 shows Citadel Securities paying Robinhood "28 cents to 42.7 cents per hundred shares" (options order-flow categories) as of October 2024, and Wolverine Execution Services "31.9 to 41.2 cents per hundred shares." This is a lower-bound proxy for wholesaler economics — it is the rebate paid to the broker, not the wholesaler's own gross spread capture, which the sources retrieved do not decompose into a clean per-contract dollar figure. S01's 12.6% average bid-ask spread on retail-preferred contracts is the best available proxy for the total spread cost borne by the retail buyer on each trade, most of which becomes wholesaler revenue net of the PFOF rebate paid out.

**How much of a retail buyer's loss is spread vs. premium decay vs. direction.** This document could **not** find a clean, published decomposition of aggregate retail 0DTE losses into these three channels. S02 documents that retail option buyers simultaneously (a) overpay implied volatility relative to subsequent realized volatility (a decay/vol-richness channel), (b) "incur enormous bid-ask spreads" (a spread channel), and (c) respond "sluggishly" to news (a direction/timing channel) — but reports a combined loss figure (5–9%, or 10–14% for high-expected-volatility announcements) without attributing specific shares of that loss to each channel. S03 similarly reports an aggregate $241,000/day retail loss figure for SPX 0DTE without a channel breakdown. **This decomposition is marked UNVERIFIED and no percentage split is asserted anywhere in this document.**

**Whether 0DTE buyers as a class have ever documented a profit.** The one clear, widely reported episode of options *buyers* profiting at the direct expense of short-gamma dealers is the GameStop (GME) event of January 2021: as GME's price rose through successive call strikes, dealers who had sold those calls were short gamma and forced to buy the underlying shares to stay delta-hedged, and that hedging-driven share buying in turn drove the stock (and the calls) higher, so long call holders profited substantially as the feedback loop ran. This episode is well documented in market commentary and in academic post-mortems of the January 2021 meme-stock episode, though it concerns single-name equity options (not SPX index 0DTE) and its transmission mechanism runs through dealers' SHARE hedging, not the option buyer's own share position — a long-only options buyer could in principle be on the winning side of such an episode without personally holding shares, but cannot engineer or reliably time it. Set against this single episode, the two most directly relevant, more rigorous, more recent studies of the actual SPX 0DTE market — S04 and S12 — both find that at the index level, 0DTE gamma exposure is balanced and "does not propagate past volatility," i.e., the dealer-short-gamma feedback loop that produced the GME outcome is explicitly **not** found to be a reliable, repeatable structural feature of the SPX 0DTE market this account would actually trade. Combined with S03's aggregate-loss finding and S02's earnings-specific loss finding, **no source retrieved documents a repeatable, systematic situation in which long-only 0DTE buyers earn money as a class**; GME-style episodes are documented but rare, single-name, and not shown to be predictable or repeatable.

**What this means given the account's constraints.** Every documented profitable role in this literature — wholesaler/market-maker spread capture (Mode 1), pre-event implied-volatility selling (Mode 2), short-premium multi-leg structuring (Mode 3), theta collection (Mode 7), and open-window liquidity provision (Mode 8) — requires SELLING options, and one (Mode 2, as typically structured around discrete announcements) typically requires holding OVERNIGHT through the event. None require holding SHARES directly, though the one documented buyer-profit episode (GME) is transmitted through *other market participants'* share hedging. A long-only, no-selling, no-spreads, no-overnight, no-shares account is, by its own rules, excluded from every documented systematic winning role identified in this review, and can at most hope to be a passive, non-repeatable beneficiary of a rare dealer-hedging episode that the most relevant recent research (S04, S12) finds is not a reliable feature of the specific market (SPX 0DTE) the account is confined to.
